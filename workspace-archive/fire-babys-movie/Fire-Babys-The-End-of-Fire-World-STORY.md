## PROLOGUE — THE DAY FIRE WORLD ALMOST ENDED

This is the day Fire World almost ended.

The day the fires went dark, and the rivers ran black. The day smoke covered the sun, and far above the clouds, from the window of the Moon Base, you could see the whole world holding its breath. Down below, in the heart of the smoke, a small hand made of flame reached out — reaching, reaching — for a small hand made of water.

But that is not where our story begins.

Our story begins long before the fire fell.

## CHAPTER ONE — THE PEACEFUL VILLAGE

Long ago, in a peaceful village, where the hearths never went dark and the river sang every morning to the shore, there stood a village called Ember Village, in the green shadow of a gentle volcano.

Every cottage had a warm window. Every street had a lantern. And in the middle of the square stood the Great Hearth, an eternal flame that the village had kept burning since before anyone could remember — a promise, passed from grandparents to grandchildren, that the light would never go out.

It was into this village, on one bright and ordinary morning, that twelve little sparks were about to arrive — and change everything.

## CHAPTER TWO — TWELVE SPARKS

Blaze came first, the way he would do everything in his life: all at once, and laughing. He popped into a cottage hearth in a shower of golden sparks, giggled once — and every lamp in the house danced along with him. A tiny star of light hovered over his head, as if the sky itself had pinned a medal on him. His mother looked into the hearth, smiled the smile of someone who has just met her favorite person, and said, "Well hello, little Blaze."

Down by the river, Aqua arrived exactly on time. She rose out of the singing water in one perfect spin, landed without a single wasted splash, and a star sparkled over her head too. When she did splash — just once, for joy — every fish in the river leaped out of the water to celebrate.

Ruko came in loud, bursting out of a pepper patch and sneezing a firework. Boulder came in gentle, rolling slowly down the grassy hill and stopping, right in the middle of the path, so that a butterfly could land on him. Sizzle came in showing off, sliding down a rainbow of sparks with his tiny bow in hand, striking a pose at the bottom like the whole village had been waiting for exactly that. And Cinder came in upside down, in a puff of blue and purple flame — and grinned about it, because Cinder always grins about it.

By the shore, Splash made waves, cannonballing a puddle like it was an ocean. Pebble made naps, curled up asleep inside an ice flower. Drift never hurried, gliding past on a leaf. Bubble never sank, floating by inside a bubble of her own, waving at everyone she passed.

On the hill above the village, two old kings watched the babies arrive: Lord Inferno of Fire, tall and warm as a bonfire at the end of a good day, and Lord Tidal of Water, deep and calm as the river at dawn.

"Twelve sparks," said Lord Inferno. "The old prophecy picked a loud bunch."

Lord Tidal chuckled, and the whole river rippled with his laugh. "Then we'd better teach them well, old friend."

And they did.

The children grew the way children do — in a blink. Blaze tried to be a hero before he could tie his shoes, wobbling through the schoolyard carrying every lunch bucket at once. "Heroes carry everything!" he announced. "Probably!" Aqua, who had been a hero of organization since birth, calmly restacked the buckets into a perfect tower and said, "Heroes also stack, Blaze." Ruko arm-wrestled a tree stump and lost, gloriously, while everyone cheered. And the village Elder taught them all the oldest rule, the way you teach a song — by heart, and together:

"Real fire is never a toy. Always get a grown-up."

On the night of the Harvest Festival, with lanterns strung across the square and ribbons of fire and water weaving together in the air above the dancers, Blaze and Aqua sat on the hill and watched the fireworks.

"Promise we'll always watch the sky together?" Aqua asked.

"Promise," said Blaze. "Fire and water. Best team ever."

It was a perfect night in a peaceful village.

And deep below it, in the dark of the earth, something old was waking up.

## CHAPTER THREE — SOMETHING WAKES BELOW

Far beneath Ember Village, where no light had ever reached, there were caverns full of oil — black and gleaming, pressed down in the dark since the world was young. And the oil had a king.

He rose out of the black lake slowly, huge and shining, with rainbows swirling on his slick skin, and he looked up at a single crack of light from the world above, and he hated it.

"So much light," said the Great Slick. "So much... noise."

Around him, his Slimers oozed out of the walls — blobs of living oil, every one of them ready to do exactly what oil does best.

"We will pour over their bright little world," said the Great Slick, "and smother it."

That night, while the village slept off the festival, one single black droplet fell from a rock into the river. The ripple spread, and spread, and spread.

In the morning, a fisherman lifted his net out of the water and frowned. The river, which sang every morning, had a strange rainbow sheen on it — beautiful, and completely wrong.

The village bell rang. The twelve heroes gathered at the Great Hearth before the two kings, and Lord Inferno's voice was grave.

"The old enemy is rising," he said. "The Smothering has begun."

## CHAPTER FOUR — THE SMOTHERING

The oil came down the river like a shadow that had learned to swim.

Splash, who solved every problem the same way, blasted it with water — and the slick splashed, and spread, and grew. "It's getting BIGGER!" he yelled, because it was. Before Aqua could make the same mistake, Lord Tidal gently stopped her hand.

"Never spray an oil fire, children," he said. "Water only spreads it."

That was the first lesson of the war, and the hardest one of all: some fires you must not fight with water.

The Slimers slid into the edges of the village, and lantern after lantern dimmed under the slick. Boulder planted himself in the road like a wall — and the oil pooled around him and held him fast, and Boulder, being Boulder, reported this calmly: "I'm okay! I'm a wall! Walls wait!" Ruko charged to the rescue, hit the oil, and ice-skated helplessly across the street into a hay cart. "New rule!" he announced from inside the hay. "The ground is soup now!"

Sizzle fired her finest spark-arrows, and every single one fizzled out on the oil. "My best shots just... fizzle?" she said, in the voice of someone whose whole plan has just been unplugged.

It was Cinder — upside-down Cinder, chaos Cinder — who saved the day by accident, the way she did most things. Scrambling away from a slick, she knocked over a festival sack of sand. The sand rained down on the oil — and the slick shrank. Cinder stared at it. Then she pointed with both hands and shouted, "Hey! The sand WORKED! I did a science!"

After that, the village had a way to fight. Street by street, the heroes learned it together: Smother it. Starve it. Sand, lids, and foam — that was the way. But above them, the Great Slick was pouring a whole river of oil down the mountain road, and smoke was clouding the sky, and in the square, the Great Hearth flickered lower than anyone had ever seen it.

"The Hearth must not go dark," said Lord Inferno.

Blaze stood before it and cupped his own small flame in his hands like a shield. "It won't," he said. "Not on my watch."

And that was when the skeletons arrived, which did not help.

The Bonehead skeleton crew came rattling down out of the sky in a junk ship, chasing anything shiny, exactly as they always did, leaking oil barrels as they went. "We come for the shiny!" cried Captain Bonehead, skidding across the deck. "...Why is the floor slippery?" What followed was the most perfect slapstick pile-up in the history of the village — skeleton over skeleton, bone over bone, one after another, until the whole crew was a single confused heap. Then a loose barrel rolled straight for the Great Hearth — and Bubble, floating serene in the middle of the chaos, wrapped it in one giant bubble at the very last second. "Got it!" she said brightly. "Please stop helping!"

## CHAPTER FIVE — THE CALL TO THE MOON

That night, the village held a council around the low-burning Hearth, and Lord Tidal unrolled an ancient map.

"The source is the Black Well, beneath the volcano," he said. "All of the oil comes from there. Stop the Well, and we stop the Smothering."

"And the Moon Base keeps the Sky Lid," said Lord Inferno, setting one heavy golden key on the map. "The one lid in all the world big enough to smother the Well."

Aqua looked up through the smoke at the moon — small, and bright, and very far away.

"Then we call the Moon Base," she said.

The quantum distress signal fired into the night sky in a pillar of blue and gold, and on the Water Moon Base, the alarm spun — and Pebble, who had slept through birthdays, battles, and one small moonquake, snapped awake instantly. "That's Blaze's signal," she said. "Move!"

The Water Babys suited up, their Cosmic Star Suits blooming over them in sleek forms of living water. At the village armory, the Fire Babys suited up too, flame becoming form, and Blaze's voice rang off the walls: "Cosmic Star Suits... on!"

Fire rockets and water rockets launched together into the starry dark.

They did not fly alone for long. A Slime Cruiser ambushed the fleet in deep space, lobbing sticky oil bombs — and when one splatted across a rocket window, Drift, the calmest archer in two worlds, wiped it away with a foam cloth without even changing expression. "Smother, don't splash," she said. "Even in space."

Then the Bonehead starfighters arrived, chasing the shiny rockets, and tangled with the Slime Cruiser, and both ships spun away in dizzy circles — nobody hurt, everybody extremely dizzy, problem accidentally solved.

In the hangar of the Moon Base, the two teams reunited, and a hologram of Lord Tidal pointed the way to a sealed chamber. The doors opened. Inside, lit like a small sunrise, stood the Sky Lid — a giant golden lid, the biggest lid in the history of lids.

Aqua looked up at it, and up, and up. "That's... a really big lid."

Blaze grinned. "Big fire. Big lid. Let's go save the world."

Boulder lifted the Lid's great hinge like it weighed nothing at all. "Light," he said. "For a mountain."

## CHAPTER SIX — THE SMALL FLAME

While the fleet loaded the Sky Lid, the Great Slick closed in on Ember Village.

The Hearth burned low. Smoke lay over the rooftops. And the villagers did the only thing there has ever been to do with a light that's going out: they crowded close around it, every one of them, and shielded it with their own tiny sparks.

Then a small child — the smallest person in the village — stepped up to the Great Hearth, cupped one tiny flame in her hands, and offered it.

"Mine's small," she said, "but it's yours."

Lord Inferno, the King of Fire, knelt down in the square so that his great crown was level with her eyes, and his voice was soft as ash and warm as coals.

"Small flames," he said, "are how the world stays lit."

## CHAPTER SEVEN — THE LID AND THE DAWN

And so the heroes came home — through the clouds, through the smoke, in a sky full of ships — to the day Fire World almost ended.

They landed at the Black Well, and the Great Slick rose to meet them like a tidal wave made of night. "Little sparks!" he thundered. "Come to watch the dark?"

Blaze and Aqua stood before the wave, very small, and did not back up one step.

"We came to put a lid on it," said Blaze.

"Literally," said Aqua. "It's a very good lid."

Then the battle began in earnest. Foam and sand rained from the fleet. Splash raised his water cannons out of pure habit, and Pebble calmly swapped them for foam: "Smother, brother." Splash's whole face lit up. "Smothering!" he declared, opening up like it was the best game ever invented. "My new favorite sport!"

Ruko and the Boneheads dropped sandbags from above — one landed on the Captain's own foot, and he hopped in circles, which the official histories record as a contribution. Cinder surfed a foam wave down the mountainside, sealing cracks as she went, shouting "Chaos, but helpful!" Sizzle's spark-arrows lit a shining path through the smoke. And across the middle of it all, steady as a table in a storm, Boulder and Pebble carried the Sky Lid. "Easy..." Boulder murmured. "Easy..."

An oil wave crashed toward the carriers, and Aqua raised a wall of water — not a spray, a shield, a wall standing between her friends and the dark. "Water protects," she said. "It doesn't have to splash."

The Lid reached the rim of the Black Well. And the Great Slick, with long drippy arms, grabbed its edge and held on. "No!" he roared. "The dark is MINE!"

Then Blaze did the bravest thing in the whole story. He didn't raise his flame. He stepped forward, unafraid — and kind — and he talked to the monster.

"You don't have to be the dark," Blaze said. "You could just... stop."

And the Great Slick hesitated.

Because for one moment, there in his shining skin, he could see his own reflection — and in the reflection, a memory. A tiny droplet of oil, all alone in the dark under the world, for a thousand years. Nobody had ever been kind to it. Nobody had ever shown it the light. Even the darkest slick began as one small drop, alone underground.

"Nobody ever showed you the light, did they?" Aqua said, gently, the way you talk to anyone who is scared.

But fear is a strong thing, and it won for one more second. With a roar, the Great Slick slammed the Sky Lid sideways. The great Lid tilted — sliding — toward the crack of lava, where, if it fell, everything was lost.

Boulder dove.

He caught the Lid's edge with both hands, his feet skidding and sparking across the stone, holding the weight of the world. "Walls..." he gritted, "...don't... drop things!"

And then everyone pushed. Fire and water. Skeletons and heroes. Villagers running up out of the smoke with buckets of sand. Every spark. Every drop. Every friend. The Lid groaned, and turned, and hung over the Black Well at last, and two small voices counted together:

"One... two... SMOTHER!"

The Sky Lid clanged down on the Black Well.

And the great wave of oil — the wave that was going to end the world — collapsed into perfect stillness.

Silence.

One full breath of silence. Then, somewhere, a single bird sang.

## CHAPTER EIGHT — THE DAWN

When oil is smothered, it sleeps. The Slimers shrank down into harmless, sleepy little droplets. And the Great Slick — huge, terrible, thunder-voiced Great Slick — was suddenly small: one round little droplet, blinking in the new quiet.

"...It's quiet," he said, in a tiny voice. "It's... not bad."

Aqua knelt down and tucked a little blanket of sand around him, the way you tuck in a baby at nap time. "Rest now," she told him. "When you wake up, we'll find you a better job."

Captain Bonehead saluted so hard his arm fell off. He put it back on backwards. "We helped!" he announced. "Mostly by falling less!"

Then the smoke tore open, and sunlight poured over Fire World, and in the village square the Great Hearth ROARED back to full flame. Lantern by lantern, window by window, the whole village lit up again. The two kings stood at the Hearth and watched their children celebrate.

"The prophecy was right," said Lord Inferno.

"It usually is, old friend," said Lord Tidal, his eyes shining like deep water. "It usually is."

That evening, at sunset, Blaze and Aqua sat on the hill and watched the sky together — exactly the way they had promised each other they always would. "Fire and water," said Aqua. "Best team ever," said Blaze, smiling at the first star. "...We should write that down somewhere."

So they did. This is it.

At the Festival of the Dawn there was dancing, and feasting, and a performance by the skeleton crew that can only be described as a dance because there is no other word in the language. Sizzle's fireworks bloomed into the shapes of all twelve heroes in the sky. Ruko finally beat the tree stump at arm-wrestling — the stump was wearing a bandage by then, and nobody asked why. Cinder fell asleep in a pile of foam, snoring tiny bubbles shaped like stars.

And on the mantle of the Great Hearth, in a small glass house where he could see everything, the tiny Slick watched the fireworks with everyone else — and smiled, just a little.

Even the dark had found a small, warm place to watch the light.

## EPILOGUE — A NEW SPARK

Fire World did not end that day.

It did not end because twelve sparks remembered the oldest rule of all — the one the Elder taught them, the way you teach a song, by heart and together:

"Real fire is never a toy. Always get a grown-up."

Some years later, in a cozy cottage in Ember Village, in a warm and well-kept hearth, a brand-new tiny spark popped into the world, giggling.

And a familiar, beloved voice said:

"Well hello, little one."

And somewhere — a river singing, a lantern lit, a story being told by firelight — a new story was already beginning.

The future is blazing for Fire Babys.

**THE END**
